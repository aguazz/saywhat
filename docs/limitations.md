# What SayWhat Cannot Do

SayWhat is a powerful tool for understanding debate structure, but it has real limits. Knowing them makes the analysis more useful, not less.

---

## It does not determine who won

A debate is not a math problem. SayWhat shows you the *structure* of the argument — which claims were challenged, which held up to fact-checking, who engaged and who evaded — but it does not declare a winner. A speaker can be technically accurate on every factual claim and still be making a bad argument. A speaker can exaggerate one figure and still have the stronger overall case. Judgment belongs to you.

---

## Verdicts are based on available evidence, not ground truth

"True" does not mean "definitively proven." It means the available evidence supports the claim. For many topics — especially anything recent, regional, contested in the scientific literature, or covered primarily in non-English sources — the evidence base the app can access is limited. The app retrieves sources from Wikipedia and Semantic Scholar. That's a reasonable starting point, but far from complete.

A verdict of **Unverifiable** often means "we couldn't find good sources," not "this claim is unknowable."

A verdict of **Partially True** often means "this is accurate but missing important context" — not that the speaker was being misleading.

---

## Confidence scores are approximate

When the app says it is "75% confident" in a verdict, that number reflects the AI model's internal assessment of how clearly the evidence supported or contradicted the claim. It is not a statistically calibrated probability derived from a test set.

Treat it as a rough signal: high confidence means the evidence was clear; low confidence means it was ambiguous or contradictory. Don't read too much into differences of a few percentage points.

---

## Claim extraction is not exhaustive

The app extracts 3–6 substantive claims per speaker turn, choosing the most significant ones. In a long, rich argument, some claims will be missed. The extraction is conservative by design — better to miss a borderline claim than to flood the analysis with noise — but important nuances can fall through the filter.

Always read the original transcript alongside the analysis for the full picture.

---

## AI-classified relationships can be wrong

Whether one claim "refutes" or merely "reframes" another is genuinely subtle. The app uses a language model that makes good judgments most of the time, but it will occasionally misclassify a response type or miss a response entirely.

If a relationship label seems wrong to you, trust your reading of the debate over the app's classification.

---

## Fallacy detection can be over-sensitive or under-sensitive

Fallacies are contextual. A rhetorical "slippery slope" in a casual debate is different from a rigorous logical error. The app tries to flag only clear examples and asks the model to show the specific quote and explain why it qualifies as a fallacy — but it can still flag a legitimate rhetorical move as a fallacy, or miss a subtle one.

Use fallacy alerts as prompts for closer reading, not as verdicts.

---

## Non-checkable claims are not scored

Moral claims, opinions, predictions, and interpretive judgments are not fact-checked and don't count toward the reliability score. A speaker who makes only philosophical or value-based arguments will show "no checkable claims" — not a high score, not a low one.

This is intentional: holding someone's values to an evidence standard would be a category error, and unfair.

---

## Language coverage is uneven

The app handles English and Spanish. Evidence retrieval is optimized for English and makes a secondary attempt in Spanish for Spain-specific queries. Claims about regional or local topics with predominantly non-English sources may receive weaker evidence support, more "Unverifiable" verdicts, and less accurate context.

---

## The graph is a simplification

A multi-hour debate with 200 claims and 80 cross-speaker responses becomes a graph. Something is inevitably lost. The graph is best used as an overview — a way to spot patterns, hotspots, and evasions — not as a complete or authoritative record of the debate.

---

## In short

Use SayWhat to *understand* a debate better, not to *settle* one. The analysis is a starting point for sharper thinking, not a substitute for it.
